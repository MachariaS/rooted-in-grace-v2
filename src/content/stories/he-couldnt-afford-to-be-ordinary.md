Denzel was twenty-four when he started feeling poor.

Not because he had nothing.

He had a job. He paid his bills. He could buy himself lunch without calling home. At the end of a good month, he even managed to save a little.

But somehow, it never felt like enough.

Every morning, before getting out of bed, he opened Instagram.

Someone had bought a car.

Someone had moved into a beautiful apartment.

Someone was on holiday in Zanzibar.

A former classmate had started a business. Another had just announced an engagement, complete with a ring that looked like it had its own insurance policy.

Denzel would scroll through it all, put his phone down and stare at his ceiling.

Then he would get up and go to work.

His salary came in at the end of the month, and for a few hours, he felt hopeful.

He paid his rent.

Sent something home.

Bought groceries.

Then he looked at what remained.

It was the same story every month.

He was working, but he wasn't moving as fast as he thought he should.

And at twenty-four, he had somehow convinced himself that time was running out.

It started with small things.

His phone was still working perfectly, but the newer model had a better camera. He could afford it if he stretched himself a little, so he bought it on credit.

His friends were going to a nice restaurant that weekend. He had planned to stay home and save, but when someone asked why he was suddenly becoming unavailable, he laughed and said he was just busy.

On Friday, he went with them.

He ordered what everyone else ordered.

When the bill arrived, he paid his share and pretended not to notice how much it had taken from his account.

The following week, he bought a pair of shoes he had been eyeing for months.

He told himself he deserved them.

And he did deserve nice things.

That wasn't the problem.

The problem was that every purchase came with a promise.

"I'll save properly next month."

"I'll start investing when my salary increases."

"I'll take that course when things settle down."

"I'll build my business when I have enough money."

There was always a next month.

There was always a better time.

And there was always someone else whose life made his own feel embarrassingly small.

One evening, Denzel met his former classmate, Daniel, at a supermarket.

Daniel was wearing a plain T-shirt, carrying a shopping basket and looking like someone who had absolutely no intention of impressing anybody.

They exchanged greetings.

"So, what are you driving these days?" Denzel asked, half-joking.

Daniel laughed.

"Nothing. I'm still using matatus."

Denzel was surprised.

Daniel had always been sharp in school. Denzel had assumed he would be one of those people who figured life out early.

"What about business? I thought you wanted to start something."

"I did. I'm saving up for it."

"Still saving?"

Daniel smiled.

"Still saving."

Denzel almost laughed.

He couldn't understand how someone could spend so long preparing to do something without feeling embarrassed about the waiting.

They talked for a few more minutes before going their separate ways.

That evening, Denzel went home thinking about the conversation.

Daniel wasn't earning much more than he was. He wasn't living some extraordinary life. He hadn't suddenly become successful.

But he seemed comfortable with where he was.

Denzel couldn't remember the last time he had felt that way.

A few months later, life gave him a small but uncomfortable lesson.

His landlord needed the rent. His phone payment was due. Then his younger sister called because she needed money for something at school.

Nothing catastrophic had happened.

It was simply life doing what life does.

Denzel opened his banking app.

He checked his balance.

Then checked it again, as if the numbers might change out of sympathy.

They didn't.

He had spent so much energy trying to look like he was progressing that he had very little room left to handle an ordinary problem.

That night, he sat on the edge of his bed with his phone in his hand.

Instagram was open.

Someone had just posted a picture of their new car.

For the first time, Denzel didn't feel jealous.

He felt tired.

Tired of comparing.

Tired of making plans that depended on a future salary.

Tired of feeling ashamed of a life that was still being built.

And then a question came to him.

"What if I stopped trying to look like I'd made it and actually gave myself the chance to make it?"

It wasn't a dramatic turning point.

The next morning, his salary hadn't increased. His phone was still on credit. His friends were still going out, and people his age were still buying cars.

But Denzel made a decision.

He would stop treating every sign of someone else's progress as evidence of his own failure.

He would pay off what he owed.

He would save what he could, even if it was a small amount.

He would take the course he had postponed for months.

He would learn to say, "I can't afford that right now," without feeling the need to explain himself.

And he would stop waiting until he could afford an impressive life to start building a meaningful one.

Some days, it was difficult.

He still compared himself to people online.

He still wondered whether he was moving too slowly.

Sometimes, he still wanted to buy something simply because he was tired of feeling ordinary.

But slowly, he began to understand something he had missed.

He wasn't behind because he hadn't bought a car at twenty-four.

He wasn't failing because he was renting a modest apartment.

He wasn't wasting his life because his achievements couldn't fit into an Instagram caption.

He was building.

And building rarely looks impressive while it is happening.

Months later, Denzel met Daniel again.

This time, Daniel told him he had finally started his business.

It was small. It wasn't making much money yet. He was still working his regular job and putting whatever he could into it.

But he had started.

Denzel congratulated him.

And for once, he didn't go home feeling like someone else's progress had taken something away from him.

He went home and worked on his own plans.

He was beginning to understand that two people could be the same age, start from similar places and still have completely different timelines.

And that was okay.

Maybe this is something we need to talk about more.

We are growing up in a world where everyone seems to be announcing something.

The new job.

The new house.

The new business.

The holiday.

The engagement.

The milestone.

And because we can see everyone's highlights, we start believing that our own lives should be producing something equally impressive every few months.

We forget that some people are building with money we cannot see.

Some are receiving help we know nothing about.

Some have been working towards the same goal for ten years.

And some are struggling behind the very pictures that make us feel inadequate.

We compare our beginnings to someone else's middle and wonder why we aren't further ahead.

We spend money to look successful.

We rush relationships because we are afraid of being left behind.

We abandon good plans because they are taking too long.

We turn ordinary seasons into personal failures because they don't look impressive enough.

And in the process, we sometimes sacrifice the very future we are trying so desperately to reach.

But delayed gratification isn't about denying yourself every good thing until you become successful.

You are allowed to enjoy your life now.

You can buy yourself something nice. You can travel when you can afford it. You can celebrate your small wins. You can have fun in your twenties without feeling guilty about it.

The question is whether the things you are enjoying today are helping you live well or quietly making tomorrow harder.

Because there is a difference between enjoying your life and constantly trying to prove that your life is worth admiring.

You don't have to postpone every joy.

But you also don't have to purchase every experience just because someone else has had it.

You don't need to look successful while you're becoming successful.

You don't need to explain why you're taking your time.

And you certainly don't need to turn your life into a race that nobody asked you to enter.

Maybe your job right now is simply to learn.

Maybe it is to save.

Maybe it is to heal, acquire a skill, build a business slowly or figure out what you actually want before committing to a life that looks good to everyone else.

Let that season do its work.

Not every season will look impressive from the outside.

Some seasons will look like saying no.

Some will look like taking the bus when you wish you could drive.

Some will look like staying home while your friends go out.

Some will look like working quietly on something nobody knows about yet.

And some will look like being twenty-four, twenty-eight or thirty and still figuring things out.

There is no shame in that.

You are not running out of time simply because your life doesn't look the way you imagined it would by now.

Give yourself permission to build something that lasts, even if it takes longer than you hoped.

One day, you may look back and realise that the season you thought was holding you back was actually teaching you how to stand.

Until then, don't spend your future trying to convince the world that you've already arrived.

You don't have to look like you've made it. Give yourself the time to actually make it.
